# Before and after

Real passages from client work, showing the rewrite rather than describing it.

## 1. Hero paragraph — export service page

**Before** (essay register, agency-centric)

> Most Indian exporters are invisible to the buyers they most want. Not because the product is wrong — because the website only speaks English, and the buyer is searching in Spanish, French, Arabic or Portuguese. We build multilingual export sites, research each market in its own language, and run the search campaigns that reach procurement teams abroad.

Three tells: the em-dash turn, "Not because X — because Y", and a rhythm that reads as written rather than said.

**After**

> We work with exporters, importers and manufacturers to build a digital presence that brings in overseas buyer enquiries. Export SEO, multilingual websites, Google Ads and lead qualification, run by one team in Bengaluru.

Plainer, faster, and it says who we work with and where we are — both things a buyer checks.

## 2. Proof section

**Before** — a four-tile stat block on a service page:

> 4 languages · 412 pages · 7.27% CTR · 7 of 7 top countries outside India

The reader is deciding whether we understand their business. This answers a question they did not ask, and every figure invites checking.

**After** — removed entirely. The same evidence moved to the linked case study, where the reader has opted in to it, and the service page earns credibility through vocabulary instead.

## 3. Pain points

**Before** (marketer's categories)

> Common challenges include lead quality issues, inefficient ad spend, poor international visibility and over-reliance on third-party marketplaces.

**After** (customer's words)

> - Enquiries arrive from countries you do not ship to.
> - Most leads are traders or students, not buyers.
> - Google Ads budget goes out every month with nothing to show for it.
> - The website is a brochure. It does not ask for an enquiry.
> - All your buyers come from one B2B portal, and the portal keeps raising its price.

Same five points. The second version gets nods.

## 4. Explaining a differentiator

**Before**

> Here's the assumption that costs Indian exporters the most money: "our buyers are procurement professionals, they all read English." Many of them do. But reading English and searching in English are completely different behaviours. A hospital administrator in Brazil who is perfectly capable of reading your English spec sheet will still type her search in Portuguese, because that is the language she thinks in. If you have no Portuguese page, you were never in that search result. She never rejected you — she never saw you.

Good writing. Wrong genre: an invented person, a flagged insight, and a closing turn.

**After**

> Most Indian exporters publish in English only. Your buyers in Latin America, Francophone Africa and the Gulf do not search in English.
>
> - Real indexable pages per language, not a translate widget
> - hreflang set up so each market gets its own page
> - Product terms researched in the local language, not translated from English
> - Enquiry routing so replies go out in the buyer's language

The argument survives. The essay does not.

## 5. Headings

**Before** — symmetrical and clever:

> Why an English-only website loses non-English buyers
> Why page speed decides whether buyers ever see you
> The SEO that actually reaches procurement teams

**After** — names the service, scannable, and matches what people search:

> Multilingual websites
> Website development for export business
> Export SEO

Note the exception: on a *guide* or blog post, the clever headings are often right, because the reader is there to read. The plain ones are for service pages, where the reader is there to evaluate. Match the heading style to why the reader came.
