# AI tells in web copy

These are the patterns that make a page read as machine-written. Most of them are *good writing* in an essay, which is why they survive review — the copy is not bad, it is the wrong genre. Run a draft against this list before showing anyone.

## 1. The setup-and-turn

A sentence that builds an expectation, then reverses it on a dash or semicolon for effect. This is the single strongest tell because AI reaches for it constantly and human marketers almost never do more than once per page.

- Before: "She did not reject you — she never saw you."
- Before: "It is not a problem to complain about. It is the opening."
- After: "If you have no page in her language, you were not in the results."

Keep at most one per page, and only if it is genuinely the sharpest way to say the thing. Zero is usually right.

## 2. The personified anecdote

Inventing a representative person to carry a point. "A procurement manager in São Paulo is searching for exactly what you sell." It feels vivid and it reads as filler to a buyer who wants to know what you do.

- Before: "A hospital administrator in Brazil who reads English perfectly will still search in Portuguese, because that is the language she thinks in."
- After: "Your buyers in Latin America do not search in English."

## 3. Em-dashes doing rhetorical work

Dashes used for emphasis and drama rather than for parenthesis. Three or more on a page is a signal. Replace with a full stop, a colon, or nothing.

- Before: "Certifications, MOQ and logistics decide the shortlist — discounts do not."
- After: "Certifications, MOQ and logistics decide the shortlist. Discounts do not."

## 4. "This is not about X. It is about Y."

Any variant of the negation-then-definition move. Just say Y.

- Before: "This is not about translation quality. It is about existence."
- After: "A translation widget does not make you findable. Only indexable pages do."

## 4b. The "X, not Y" headline

The comma-contrast used as a *heading*. It is the same reversal as the setup-and-turn wearing different punctuation, and it is the most common shape in AI-written marketing headings because it sounds decisive while committing to nothing.

- Before: "One team, not four vendors"
- After: "One team runs all of it"
- Before: "Revenue, not vanity metrics"
- After: "Can they tell you what a lead costs?"
- Before: "Do they optimise for AI search, not just Google?"
- After: "Do they optimise for AI answers as well as Google?"

Adjacent shapes to watch, all magazine-headline register rather than web copy:

- "A different kind of [X]" — says nothing; name the difference instead
- "Every [X] that [verb]s a [Y] — under one roof" — the dash-plus-slogan construction
- "[Noun] built for [abstract noun]" — "built for revenue", "engineered for growth", "designed for scale"

In body prose an occasional "not" contrast is ordinary writing and reads fine — one or two on a page is human. In a heading it is nearly always the tell. The heading's job is to name the section so a scanner can find it, not to land a point.

## 5. Flagging your own good point

"That last point is worth dwelling on." "Here is the assumption that costs the most money." "One useful side effect." If the point is good, the reader will notice. If it needs announcing, cut it.

## 6. Rule of three, everywhere

Three-item lists, three-clause sentences, three-part structures. One or two per page is natural. When every list has exactly three items, it reads as generated.

## 7. Hedged intensifiers

"genuinely", "remarkably", "considerably", "dramatically", "arguably", "notably", "significantly". They add emphasis without adding information, and they cluster in AI prose.

- Before: "AI traffic converts at a dramatically higher rate."
- After: Give the number, or drop the claim.

## 8. Essay connectives

"That said", "What's more", "Crucially", "Importantly", "It is worth noting". These stitch an argument together. Web copy does not need stitching because it is scanned, not read linearly.

## 9. Balanced rhythm

Every paragraph the same length, every sentence a similar shape. Real copy is lumpy — a four-word sentence next to a long one. If a page has no short sentences, it was not written by a person in a hurry, and business copy should read like it was.

## 10. Abstractions where a concrete noun exists

- Before: "digital visibility and lead qualification"
- After: "ranking for the searches your buyers run, and filtering out the enquiries that waste your sales team's time"

## 11. Symmetrical headings

"Where buyers look", "Why English fails", "What speed decides". Clever, parallel, and useless for scanning or search. Name the thing: "Export SEO", "Website development for export business".

## 12. The closing lift

A final paragraph that zooms out to significance. "That is what it looks like when a site stops being a brochure and starts being findable." End on the CTA instead.

---

## Review pass

Read the draft once looking only for these. Count the em-dashes. Count the setup-and-turns. Read the headings alone and check they name services. Read the first sentence of each paragraph and check none of them announces its own importance.

Then read the page as someone in the industry who is short of time. If a sentence would not change whether they enquire, it is decoration.
