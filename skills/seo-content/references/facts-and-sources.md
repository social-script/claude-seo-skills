# Facts and sources

A page earns trust, rankings and AI citations through facts a reader can check. This file covers what counts as a fact, how to source it, and the extra rules for regulated industries.

## Where facts may come from

In order of preference:

1. **The client's own material:** price list, catalogue, case results, Search Console, analytics, the people named on the page. Note the file or person and the date.
2. **Our own work and data:** what we built, what we measured (rank checks with the date checked, Search Console exports), screenshots we captured.
3. **Primary sources:** laws, regulations, gazette notifications, official platform policies (Google, Meta), standards bodies, peer-reviewed papers. Link the primary document, not a blog about it.
4. **Reputable secondary sources:** named studies with a method (Ahrefs, Nielsen Norman Group), established news outlets, law-firm explainers. Name them in the sentence and link them.

Never use: "studies show", "experts say", "industry data suggests", round numbers with no source, or statistics copied from another agency's blog.

## Rules

- **Every statistic links to its source.** If you cannot find one, cut the number or rephrase as our own observation ("across the clinics we work with…"), and only if that is true.
- **Client claims are checkable.** Rankings say where and when they were checked ("Bengaluru, 19 Oct 2026"). Results the client has not agreed to publish stay out. Some clients prefer "leads doubled" to exact numbers; follow what they approve.
- **Credentials must be exact.** Qualifications, memberships and certifications go on the page only as the client states them, and are re-checked with the client before publishing. (A wrong credential once had to be removed from 92 places on a client site.)
- **Prices and dates stay current.** A cost page carries the date its prices were confirmed. Add a review date to the brief.
- **Say when there is no answer.** If a common question has no authoritative answer (e.g. a "rule" with no source), say so. That is useful and citable.
- **General claims need either a source or a softer form.** "Most patients start on Google" needs a source; "patients usually start on Google or Maps, in our experience across clinics" is honest without one.

## Authors and dates

- Every guide and blog post has a real author with an author page (name, role, background, photo, LinkedIn). Google's guidance asks who wrote content and why they are qualified. Do not publish under a name that is not a real person.
- Show the published date and, when the content materially changes, the updated date. Do not bulk-change dates without changing content.

## Regulated industries

Some industries restrict what may be said in marketing. Check before writing, and cite the rule on the page when it helps the reader.

- **Dental (India):** the Revised Dentists (Code of Ethics) Regulations, 2014, applied by the National Dental Commission since March 2026. Websites must be factual and may show treatments and fees (8.2.9). Forbidden: claims of superiority, "best" or "first" (8.2.7, 8.2.8), boasting of cases or cures (8.2), inducements and rebates (8.1.3), "specialist" without a recognised qualification (8.3.3). The NDC advisory of 14 Oct 2026 warns against patient testimonials, before-and-after photos and recording procedures. Sources: https://dciindia.gov.in/Rule_Regulation/Gazette_Notification_reg_DCI_Revised_Dentists_Code_of_Ethics_Regulations_2014_27.06.2014.pdf and https://dciindia.gov.in/Admin/NewsArchives/M-2672.pdf
- **Medical (India):** similar restrictions apply to doctors under the National Medical Commission's professional conduct rules. Check the current regulations before writing.
- **Health ads (Google, Meta):** health is a sensitive interest; no remarketing audiences built on it (Google). No ad copy that implies the reader has a condition ("Suffering from tooth pain?") (Meta).
- **Financial advice:** check the regulator for the country (SEBI in India, ASIC in Australia) before making any claim about returns or advice.
- **Australia, consumer law:** the ACCC treats unsupported claims and fake reviews as misleading conduct.

When in doubt, write the factual version and flag the question to the client.
